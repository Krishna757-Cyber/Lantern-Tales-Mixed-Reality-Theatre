> **Dedication Card**:

## 1. Historical & Community Origins

**Kathputli** (from *kāth* = wood and *putli* = doll) is one of the oldest known string-puppet traditions in South Asia, originating in the Marwar, Shekhawati, and Mewar regions of Rajasthan, India. 

For over a millennium, the tradition has been preserved and performed by hereditary puppeteer families belonging to the **Bhat community** (traditionally residing in villages around Nagaur, Churu, and Jaipur). The Bhat artists were historically nomadic, travelling during the dry months from village to village and performing under the patronage of feudal chieftains (*Thakurs*) and royal durbars, before returning to their ancestral settlements during the monsoon.

## 2. Construction, Materials & Aesthetics

1. **The Head & Neck**:
   - Carved from a single piece of lightweight seasoned wood—traditionally *Rohida* (*Tecomella undulata*, desert teak) or mango wood.
   - Distinctive stylized facial features: large expressive almond-shaped eyes outlined with black kohl (*kajal*), stylized curved moustaches for male figures, painted arched eyebrows, and prominent aquiline noses.
   - The head is painted with natural or oil pigments: warm ochre/wheatish for courtiers, ivory for noble heroines, or black/deep hues for acrobats and tricksters.

2. **The Torso & Limbs**:
   - The upper torso is made of stuffed cotton and old cloth fragments bound tightly with twine.
   - Arms are made of layered, stitched cloth with stuffed cotton wrists and small carved wooden hands. Elbows are flexible cloth pivots.
   - Traditional Kathputli do **not** have carved legs (with exceptions like acrobats or horse riders). Instead, female and court puppets have long, pleated skirts (*Ghaghra*) made of vibrant multi-tiered bandhani or block-printed cotton that flow to the ground. When the puppeteer swirls the puppet, the weight of the flared hem creates an illusion of dancing feet.

3. **Costumes & Embellishments**:
   - Sourced from traditional Rajasthani textiles: block-printed cotton (*Dabu*, *Bagru*), tie-dye (*Bandhani*), and satin brocade.
   - Trimmed with sparkling metallic ribbon (*Gota Patti*), gold tinsel, and tiny mirrors (*Abhala / Shisha work*) that catch footlight flickers.
   - Male figures wear traditional turbans (*Pagri* / *Safaa*) wound with gold thread and flared tunics (*Angrakha*); female figures wear a veil (*Odhni* or *Chunari*).

4. **Strings & Manipulation**:
   - Strings (*Taag*) are spun from heavy black or unbleached cotton cord.
   - Unlike Western marionettes with complex wooden cross-bars (crutches), the traditional Bhat puppeteer uses an ingenious minimalist control: 
     - A loop of string is tied directly around the puppeteer's fingers or over a small wooden peg (*Khatola*).
     - Just 2 to 4 primary strings run from the puppet: one anchored to the crown of the head, two running to the wooden hands/wrists, and one tethered to the lower back/waist for bows, backbends, and pirouettes.
     - With finger twitches and wrist rotations, the puppeteer creates rapid spinning turns (*Chakkar*), salutations (*Salaam*), and rhythmic foot-tapping.

## 3. The Acoustic World: Boli, Dholak & Manjira

- **The Boli (or Ghona)**:
  - The voice of the puppet is entirely non-human and symbolic. The lead puppeteer places a small reed whistle called a **Boli** (made of two thin strips of bamboo or metal bound around a rubber or reed membrane) against the roof of the mouth.
  - Blowing through the Boli produces high-pitched, warbling, bird-like squeaks, whistles, and expressive trills.
  - In traditional performance, the puppet "speaks" through the boli, and the accompanist (traditionally the puppeteer's wife or partner playing the dholak) interprets and dialogues with the puppet, translating its complaints, jokes, and songs into spoken Rajasthani/Hindi dialect.
  - **In Our Virtual Theatre**: We strictly avoid synthesized human speech or caricature accents. We procedurally synthesize the authentic acoustic profile of the *boli* reed (bandpass-filtered sawtooth with microtonal glides linked to head tilt) paired with universal iconographic paper speech-bubbles.

- **Rhythm & Percussion**:
  - The stage is driven by the double-headed **Dholak** drum and brass hand cymbals (**Manjira**).
  - Folk rhythmic cycles (*Kaharwa* 8-beat, *Dadra* 6-beat, and fast *Laggi* crescendos) announce entrances, accentuate dancing spins, and create dramatic halts.

## 4. Sacred vs. Secular Repertoire (Cultural Guidelines)

> **CRITICAL ETHICAL DIRECTIVE**:  
> In court Kathputli tradition, puppets are secular folk entertainment and oral history tools, distinctly separate from sacred temple icon worship or sacred scroll-recitation traditions (such as the *Phad* scrolls of Pabuji and Devnarayan).  
> **Rule**: Under NO circumstances should Hindu deities, Muslim saints, or sacred devotional ceremonies be depicted as interactive playthings or mocked.

### Secular Folk Repertoire Selected for Lantern Tales:
1. **Anarkali Nritya (The Court Dancer)**:
   - Celebrates the grace of Kathak-infused court dance: graceful wrist salutations (*Salaam*), swift spins (*Chakkars*), and ghungroo bell rhythms.
2. **Ghodi aur Savar (The Horse and Rider)**:
   - Demonstrates the puppeteer's virtuosic manipulation of a two-part rig: a wooden horse prancing and bowing, ridden by a turbaned warrior who salutes the audience.
3. **Sapera (The Snake Charmer)**:
   - A traditional musical folk act: a charming tune played on the *been* (gourd flute), while a stylized cloth serpent emerges peacefully from its basket, swaying to the melody and bowing before returning to rest. Completely non-violent and gentle.
4. **Amar Singh Rathore (The Folk Hero of Nagaur)**:
   - A revered legendary 17th-century folk hero whose courage, integrity, and defiance of tyranny is the centerpiece of the Bhat repertoire. Presented here as a dignified, symbolic staging celebrating loyalty and honor without graphic battle or bloodshed.
5. **Mela Night (Carnival Finale)**:
   - Celebrates the joy of village fairs (Pushkar, Nagaur) with acrobatics, rhythmic duets, and joyful community celebration.
